AI BIAS & FAIRNESS LAB · A GUIDED INTRODUCTION

What even is AI bias?

A system can make decisions that repeatedly disadvantage some people—even when nobody tells it to.

Imagine being qualified for a job, but getting filtered out because the scoring system favors people who resemble previous hires. Your skills did not change. The system used the wrong clue.

Why should I care?

Automated decisions can affect who gets an interview, whose content gets recommended, or whose request gets extra scrutiny. An impressive average score can hide the people a system keeps getting wrong.

What you’re looking at: invented examples with visible arithmetic. This page does not run a trained AI model. The scores and groups below are teaching choices, not findings about real people or hiring products.

LESSON 01 · THE BASIC IDEA

Same skills. Different answer.

Two fictional applicants each earn 78 skill points out of 100. Our deliberately flawed rule subtracts points from Group B for an unrelated background clue. Should that change who gets an interview?

A

Applicant A

Job-relevant skills: 78 points
Background deduction: 0 points

78 − 0 = 78

B

Applicant B

Job-relevant skills: 78 points
Background deduction: 25 points

78 − 25 = 53

This penalty is invented on purpose so you can see its effect. It is not an estimate of real-world discrimination.

← Decrease: take fewer points away.Increase →: take more points away.

Why try this? The clue tells us nothing about ability in this example. Removing it should leave the two equally skilled applicants with equal scores.

Where do these numbers come from?

We chose 78 skill points, a starting 25-point deduction, and a starting interview cutoff of 70. They are round teaching values. A score is points on this toy scale, not a probability of being qualified. No applicant data was collected and no real model was audited.

In real systems, hiding a name may not remove other clues linked to group membership. This demonstration removes one explicit penalty; it does not prove that blind screening solves fairness generally.

WHAT YOU JUST LEARNED

Equal skills can lead to different decisions if the system uses an irrelevant clue. That can happen even without an explicitly hostile instruction.

LESSON 02 · FOLLOW THE PEOPLE, NOT JUST THE PERCENTAGE

“95% accurate” can hide who gets missed.

Now apply the same scoring rule to 1,000 fictional applicants. In each group, half are labeled qualified for this toy exercise. We know those labels because we created the example.

A score at or above this line earns an interview. This changes the decision rule, not anyone’s skills.

← Lower: more people pass, including potentially unqualified applicants.Higher →: fewer people pass, including potentially qualified applicants.

This changes who is counted in the overall average. It does not retrain a model or repair the group penalty.

← Toward 50%: the groups count equally in the average.Toward 90% →: Group A dominates the average.

HEADLINE ACCURACY—

Who actually gets an interview?

Show every count and the metric formulas
Every simulated decision, grouped by the label we assigned.
DecisionGroup AGroup B
Overall accuracy
Correct interviews plus correct rejections, divided by all 1,000 applicants. A “correct” decision matches the qualification label built into this example.
Qualified-applicant interview rate
Qualified applicants invited ÷ all qualified applicants in that group. Technical name: true positive rate, or recall.
Opportunity gap
The difference between those two qualified-applicant interview rates, shown in percentage points. It describes this one difference, not whether a whole system is fair.
Selection-rate ratio

These metric definitions follow Fairlearn’s assessment guide. No single number is a fairness certificate. Who is affected, what errors cost, and how qualification labels were created also matter.

What exactly is this simulation doing?

Each group starts with the same 100-record template: 50 records labeled qualified and 50 labeled unqualified. Qualified skill scores are evenly spaced between 70 and 98; unqualified scores between 5 and 48. The Group B penalty is subtracted before comparing each score with the cutoff. We repeat the template to reach 1,000 applicants with the group mix you selected.

Those ranges deliberately make the original skill score highly predictive, so you can isolate the added penalty. Real qualification labels and model scores are much messier. This code does not train an AI, perform class weighting, or run SMOTE. Changing group mix changes the evaluation average, not training representation.

LESSON 03 · PATTERNS ARE NOT FACTS

Common in examples doesn’t mean true about a person.

A word predictor can favor patterns that appear often in its examples. Here’s a deliberately tiny counting exercise: change how often three pronouns appear, then see their shares of the example text.

This is not an LLM benchmark. These are invented word counts, not measured probabilities from ChatGPT or any other model, and not statistics about any occupation.

Example sentence: “The engineer said that ___ would review it.”

Increase a word’s count and its share grows. Decrease it and its share shrinks. Changing one count also changes the total used to calculate every share.

Share of this example text

A real language model uses learned relationships and context, not this three-word counting rule. Still, the exercise raises a useful question: is an output reflecting evidence about this person, or a familiar pattern? Word frequency alone cannot tell us someone’s gender, ability, or whether a system treats people fairly.

WHAT YOU CAN NOW ASK

“Where did this result come from? Who does it work for? What changed when I moved that control?” Those questions are more useful than accepting a “fairness fixed” badge.

TAKE IT FURTHER · TERMS YOU MAY RUN INTO

What can engineers actually change?

There isn’t a universal “make it fair” switch. Different approaches change different parts of a system. Start with what the method does, then ask whether it helps the people affected.

BEFORE LEARNING

Improve the examples

If relevant cases are missing or labels reflect past unfair decisions, review the data and how it was collected. More examples do not automatically mean better examples.

What is SMOTE?

SMOTE creates synthetic training examples between nearby records in an underrepresented class. A class might mean “fraud,” not a demographic group. It can help some imbalanced classification tasks, but it can also make poor examples. It is not a fairness guarantee and is not implemented in this page.

DURING LEARNING

Change which mistakes cost more

During training, an error contributes to a model’s loss—a number it tries to reduce. Class weighting makes errors on selected classes count more. For example, missing a rare fraud case could cost more than another error.

Does that mean the AI is punished?

No feelings, rewards, or money are involved. A weight changes the arithmetic used to fit the model. It may change which patterns the model learns, so engineers must test both the intended improvements and any new mistakes. Class weighting and demographic fairness are different questions.

WHEN DECIDING

Change the cutoff, then check the tradeoff

A lower interview cutoff invites more people. Some are qualified people previously missed; some may not meet the example’s qualification label. Lesson 02 shows both counts instead of calling either direction automatically “fair.”

What else should be checked?

Check group results, incorrect acceptances, missed qualified applicants, and whether the labels themselves are justified. Keep human review and an appeal path appropriate to the real setting. One small metric gap does not settle every fairness concern.

The takeaway

AI bias is about patterns of outcomes and their consequences. Ask what was measured, inspect who gets missed, and test whether a change helps without hiding a new problem.
